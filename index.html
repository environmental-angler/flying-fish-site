The problem

The culvert gets replaced. The question stays open.

On the ground

Undersized crossings cut streams in two

Perched and narrow culverts block brook trout, salmon, river herring and eels, and they wash out in big storms. Many towns have dozens and money for a few.

For your team

Short staff, long checklists

Grant timelines, permit windows and design reviews leave little time for careful field data, and almost none for going back after construction.

What's fair

Public money should come with proof

When a community spends on fish passage, it deserves to know whether fish are passing. Funders increasingly ask the same thing.

How it works

Three steps, from barrier to proof.

You can hire us for one step or all three. Each one hands off cleanly to your engineer, contractor or funder.

  1. Assess

    Survey crossings to the NACC protocol, score barriers, and rank which ones to fix first. For design, we survey reference reaches to match the new crossing to the stream.

    You get: scored crossing data, a ranked list, design-ready survey data

  2. Document

    Record conditions before, during and after construction with ground photos and drone imagery, and check the as-built against the design.

    You get: dated photo record, orthomosaics, as-built memo

  3. Verify

    Show that the crossing works: re-survey the channel, then confirm fish presence and passage with counters, eDNA, telemetry or electrofishing.

    You get: effectiveness report written for funders

Why it matters

What a verified crossing is worth

With field data and follow-up

  • Your grant reports show fish passage, not just a finished pipe.
  • Your next application is stronger because the last one delivered.
  • Problems are caught while the contractor can still fix them.
  • Miles of upstream habitat open for spawning and cold-water refuge.

Without them

  • A new crossing can still block fish if grade or embedment is off.
  • Nobody finds out until the next assessment, years later.
  • Funders see spending, with no measured result.

About

Mike Manning, fisheries biologist

I spent my first career as an Air Force flight medic and a civilian firefighter and paramedic. After graduate school I came to fisheries, and I've spent the last several years working on rivers in Downeast Maine: fishways, fish counters, telemetry and stream crossings.

I started Flying Fish because the same question kept coming up after crossings were rebuilt: did it work? Answering it takes field skills that sit between engineering, biology and data. That's the work I like best.

I work alone much of the time and bring the safety habits of my first career to cold water and roadside sites. When a project needs a larger crew, I team with partners I trust.
